03 the ask
what you can point it at

Anything outside your head that keeps changing.

Atlas watches things in the world and tells you what moved. It can't read your inbox or your connected apps, and it isn't a flowchart builder. Open one of these to see the sentence you'd actually write.

The weather where you live, before you head out

Every morning, tell me the forecast for Boston — rain, temperature, and whether I need a coat.

A price you're waiting to see drop

Every evening, check this page and tell me if the price fell below what I said I'd pay.

A stock or a coin you're watching

Every weekday after the close, tell me how it moved and what was behind the move.

A website, the moment anything on it changes

Every hour, check this page and tell me what changed — quote the part that's new.

Your team's score and injury list

Every Monday, give me the result, the table position, and who's out.

A question you keep looking up

Every Friday, tell me what changed in the thing I'm tracking this week, with a source for each claim.

If you can say it in a sentence, Atlas can watch it.

04 how it works
three beats, nothing to configure

The sentence is the task.

One line contains both what you want and when you want it. Atlas pulls the schedule out of the sentence and keeps the rest as the prompt. There is no second screen.

beat 01

You write one sentence

Every morning, tell me the forecast for Boston — rain, temperature, and whether I need a coat.

The tinted part is the schedule. The underlined part is the prompt. You don't mark either one — Atlas does.

beat 02

It separates

schedule · daily 07:00 prompt

The schedule becomes a cadence. The rest becomes the question it asks the model, every time, unchanged.

beat 03

The result lands

RE: BOSTON MORNING FORECAST — RUN 001

High 68°F, low 54°F. Rain likely 2pm–6pm, 70% chance — take the coat. Wind 12mph NE. Sunrise 05:31.

checked: has a source, nothing missing

Graded first, then sent. Free gets one digest a day at an hour you pick; Pro gets every run as it lands.

Schedules

Ten cadences, drawn to size

One surface, ten schedules. The faster a schedule runs, the smaller and more numerous the bodies it leaves behind; the slower it runs, the heavier the single body that settles. Four cadences are free. Six more come with Pro, and they are the fast ones.

05 the check
the part nobody else does

A wrong answer arrives looking exactly like a right one.

This is how these tools fail quietly: a confident number arrives in the same email, at the same time, looking exactly like a good one. So Atlas grades every run before it reaches you — blank replies, refusals, answers cut off mid-sentence, claims with no source. Each gets its own tag in your run history.

Six ways a run comes back wrong

Pick a tag to read it

okThe run finished and nothing tripped a check. It goes to you as it came back.

emptyThe reply came back blank. It still arrives on time, which is the problem.

refusalThe model declined to answer. You get the refusal, not a result.

truncatedThe answer stops mid-sentence. The part you did get can still look finished.

loopThe answer keeps restating itself instead of getting anywhere.

unsourcedA claim arrives with nothing behind it. No link, no quote, nothing to check.

After 3 consecutive flagged runs, the task auto-pauses. You do not have to be the one who notices.

runs merging cleanly three flagged in a row — they jam channel dry — the task paused itself

Runs merge cleanly until three are flagged in a row. Those three jam against each other, and the channel past them runs dry — the task paused itself before it could send you three more answers it did not trust.

Auto-pause notice

boston-morning-forecast — paused after 3 consecutive flagged runs.

You hear about it once a week at most — one click snoozes it for thirty days, and the first good run clears the flag on its own. Nothing here nags.

07 comparison
where atlas is the wrong tool

Zapier and Make are better than Atlas at most of this.

Atlas against Zapier, Make and n8n

Two ways over the same step

Zapier and Make are visual editors with large connector libraries, and both now ship AI assistance too. Atlas starts from the other end: describe the outcome, AI works out how. Less setup for open-ended, judgement-heavy tasks — and far fewer integrations than they have.

If your automation is a fixed chain between specific apps, they are the better fit — that is good engineering, and it will outlast anything I ship. Atlas is for the other kind of job: watch this, and tell me what matters. Atlas is one person, and the connector list looks like it.
