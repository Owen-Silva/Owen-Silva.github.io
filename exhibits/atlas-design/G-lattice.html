03

The ask

What you can point it at.

Every task is a version of the same request: watch this, tell me what matters. Six of the shapes that takes.

  • 01 The weather where you live, before you head out
  • 02 A price you're waiting to see drop
  • 03 A stock or a coin you're watching
  • 04 A website, the moment anything on it changes
  • 05 Your team's score and injury list
  • 06 A question you keep looking up

If you can say it in a sentence, Atlas can watch it.

Worth stating up front: Atlas watches things that are out in the open — pages, prices, feeds, forecasts. It is not a flowchart builder and not an integrations marketplace, and it cannot read your inbox, your calendar, or anything else behind your login. If the answer already lives inside your own accounts, this is the wrong tool.

04

How it works

What happens to the sentence.

Three steps. You do the first one.

  1. 01 Sentence in

    You write it the way you'd say it.

    The whole task

    Every morning, tell me the forecast for Boston — rain, temperature, and whether I need a coat.

    What you want and when you want it are both in there. There is no second screen where the real configuration happens. This is the configuration.

  2. 02 Schedule parsed

    The schedule is read out of it.

    “Every morning” is not a field you fill in afterwards. Atlas pulls it out of what you already wrote and matches it to one of ten fixed frequencies. No picker, no cron expression. There are only ten, so the sentence gets rounded to the closest one.

    Parsed schedule

    “Every morning” becomes → DAILY 07:00

  3. 03 Result delivered

    It arrives in an email, graded.

    Atlas runs the prompt, then reads the answer back before you do — did it actually answer, did it cite anything, is something missing — and puts what it found at the bottom.

    Free gets one digest a day, at an hour you pick. Per-run email is Pro. So on free, a task that finishes at 07:04 waits for the digest instead of interrupting you. That is worse if you wanted the alert. It is better if you did not want ten emails.

    RE: BOSTON MORNING FORECAST — RUN 001

    High 68°F, low 54°F. Rain likely 2pm–6pm, 70% chance — take the coat. Wind 12mph NE. Sunrise 05:31.

    — checked: has a source, nothing missing

    That last line is Atlas grading its own output. It is not a second opinion.

05

The check

A wrong answer looks exactly like a right one.

This is how these tools fail quietly: a confident number arrives in the same email, at the same time, looking exactly like a good one. So Atlas grades every run before it reaches you — blank replies, refusals, answers cut off mid-sentence, claims with no source. Each gets its own tag in your run history.

Six ways a run fails, and what each one looks like.

  • ok

    The answer came back whole, and it cited something.

    Complete cell. Every strut carries.

  • empty

    Nothing came back. A blank reply is still a reply.

    A void — the cell exists, the structure inside it does not.

  • refusal

    The model declined. That is an answer about the model, not the question.

    Capped. The path is blocked at the node.

  • truncated

    It stopped mid-sentence. The end of the answer is missing.

    A severed strut. The load has nowhere left to go.

  • loop

    It restated itself until it ran out of room.

    A closed circuit. Nothing leaves the node it started at.

  • unsourced

    It made a claim and pointed at nothing.

    Unanchored — struts that reach for a node that is not there.

07

Against the others

Most of the time, they are the better tool.

Zapier, Make and n8n are visual editors with large connector libraries. Zapier and Make now ship AI assistance of their own, too. Atlas did not invent this category and does not lead it.

Zapier · Make · n8n

You pick the apps, wire the steps, and the same path runs every time. That is a real advantage: it is exact, it is inspectable, and somebody other than you can open it and see what it does.

It is also a single load path. Change one app, or lose one step, and the chain stops. You accept that because you already know the shape of the job.

Atlas

Atlas starts from the other end. One English sentence carries the request and the schedule together; the model works out how, run after run. It reads its own answer back, tags it, and pauses the task after three bad ones in a row.

Less setup for open-ended, judgement-heavy work. And, honestly, far fewer integrations than they have — Atlas reads, decides and emails. It does not wire your apps together.

The short version

If your automation is a fixed chain between specific apps, they are the better fit. If it is “watch this and tell me what matters,” that is Atlas.

Use them instead when…

  • 01The trigger and the destination are both specific apps, and the steps between them never change.
  • 02You need a connector Atlas does not have, which is most connectors.
  • 03Somebody other than you has to open the automation and understand it without asking you first.
  • 04It has to run more often than once a day and you are not paying for anything. Atlas’s free floor is daily.
  • 05You need an API, a mobile app, or single sign-on. Atlas has none of the three.
